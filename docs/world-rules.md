# World rules

The accuracy and art constraints every picture and every case in Behold is checked against. Read before writing a case or generating a picture. Checked at two points: when a picture is accepted into a case, and when a case is reviewed against its passage.

Two principles sit above everything here.

**The text decides.** Every tappable object in a scene is named or implied by the passage. Set dressing is generic and never carries a clue. Captions and in-world documents paraphrase scripture in the game's own voice; scripture appears verbatim only in the reveal, fetched from the translation service, never baked into the app.

**The picture is evidence.** A player must be able to read what matters from the picture alone, at arm's length, on a phone. Legibility outranks beauty; beauty outranks everything else.

## 1. Time and place

**Tutorial — the valley of Elah.** Judah, c. 1010 BC. Iron Age I/II transition. Philistine and Israelite armies; bronze armor still common, iron weapons present.

**Season one — the house of Ahab.** The northern kingdom of Israel, c. 874–841 BC, Iron Age II. Places: Samaria (the capital, hilltop, ashlar masonry, the "ivory house" of 1 Kings 22:39), Jezreel (the second royal residence and its vineyard), Mount Carmel and the Kishon brook, Ramoth-gilead (east of the Jordan), Shunem (a village in the Jezreel valley), Damascus (Aram, Naaman's country), the Jordan at Jericho, and the road up to Jezreel from the Jordan valley. Assyrian records credit Ahab with a large chariot force; chariots are normal royal transport in this season.

**Season two — Joseph.** Canaan and Egypt, Middle Kingdom-to-Second-Intermediate Egypt. Written when season two is designed; Egyptian material culture is a separate document.

## 2. Material world (season one)

**Architecture.** Fieldstone foundations and mudbrick walls, plastered; flat roofs with parapets and an outside stair; upper rooms on the roof (2 Kings 4:10). Pillared houses with a central court. City walls with casemates and towers; gates with side chambers, a heavy timber lintel, and wooden doors. Royal buildings in dressed ashlar with ivory-inlaid furniture. Balconies and latticed windows on upper stories (2 Kings 1:2; 9:30).

**Furniture and objects.** Low tables, stools, benches, couches with cushions; a wealthy guest room holds a bed, a table, a chair, and a lamp — the four items 2 Kings 4:10 lists. Oil lamps are shallow clay saucers with a pinched spout (the Behold icon). Storage jars, wineskins, baskets, woven mats, bronze bowls. Writing on papyrus with a reed pen, or on potsherds; letters folded and sealed with a clay bulla pressed by a stamp seal (many carried the owner's name); scrolls, never bound books.

**Money and weight.** No coins — coinage begins two and a half centuries later. Silver is weighed: cut pieces, rings, and small bars in a pouch, with a balance scale and stone weights nearby. "Its worth in money" in a caption means weighed silver in the picture. A talent is a load a man carries with effort; two talents in two bags need two men (2 Kings 5:23).

**Arms and armor.** Bronze scale armor (overlapping plates sewn to leather), bronze helmets, leather or bronze greaves, round or oblong shields, spears with iron heads, straight swords in scabbards, composite bows, slings. Chariots with two horses and a driver plus a fighter. **Never chain mail** — it is centuries later.

**Clothing.** Wool and linen. Men: knee- to ankle-length tunics belted with a sash, a fringed mantle over the shoulder, sandals, a plain cloth headband or head-cloth. Women: ankle-length tunics, a shawl or head covering, sandals. Royalty and the wealthy: dyed red and purple, gold trim and jewelry, a thin gold circlet or diadem as the mark of a king — never a medieval spiked crown. Commoners wear no gold. Servants and laborers wear plain undyed cloth. Prophets are not glowing, haloed, or robed in white by default.

**Food.** Flat bread, parched grain, figs, raisins, olives and oil, grapes and wine, cheese, lentils; meat is for feasts and sacrifice.

**Animals.** Donkeys for ordinary riding and burden; horses for chariots and for mounted messengers (2 Kings 9:17–19 sends two horsemen out to meet Jehu), never for farm work; oxen, sheep and goats, pariah dogs at the edges of towns, ravens, camels only for foreign traders.

**Landscape.** Terraced hillsides, vineyards with low stone walls and watchtowers, olive groves, cypress and oak, dry summer ground and a wet winter; Carmel green above a brown plain; the Jordan valley hot and low.

## 3. Anachronisms — the catch list

Regenerate any picture containing: crosses in any form · Greek or Roman columns, capitals, arches, or togas · pointed or rounded gate arches (use lintels) · coins · chain mail · stirrups on any rider · medieval crowns · glass windows · bound books or modern paper (papyrus and potsherds are right) · halos, wings on people, glowing figures · turbans and costume from later Islamic-era art · tattoos on Israelites · modern breeds of dog or horse · text or lettering of any kind, including on banners and seals.

## 4. People

Levantine skin tones and features; adult Israelite men are bearded (a shaved man is in mourning, or an Egyptian); hair dark and worn short to shoulder-length. Plain headbands on commoners. Ages read clearly: a boy is a boy, an elder is grey. Crowds are individuals in plain cloth, faces mostly turned to the scene.

**The marker rule.** Every recurring character carries one marker that reads at thumbnail size and appears in every picture of them — this is how a player tracks a person across moments, and it is the answer to the Obra Dinn problem of confusable figures. Markers come from the text where the text gives one.

Cast sheets are generated once per character on a plain unlit background, front view, full body, and attached to every scene prompt that includes the character.

## 5. Depiction rules

Violence is implied, never shown: the stones before, the stain after; Jezebel's window and the dogs, not the body. No blood on a living person. No sexual content of any kind; where a passage turns on it (season three), the scene is the trial or the evidence, never the act.

The LORD is never depicted — no face, figure, hand, or voice-line in a picture. Fire from heaven is fire; a cloud is a cloud. Angels do not appear in season one. Miracles are shown as their evidence: the drenched altar burning, the jar that is still full, the child sitting up.

Prophets are men in ordinary dress with their markers. Kings are men in fine dress with a circlet. Nobody glows.

## 6. The style bible

The look is locked from the first accepted pictures: flat, clean illustration with bold shapes and strong contrast, "a picture book for adults." Scenes are portrait, 4:5. Warm daylight for day scenes, one warm lamp against blue night for interiors. No text anywhere in an image.

Every generation prompt begins with this header, verbatim, with the period clause swapped per season (the tutorial's reads "Ancient Israel around 1000 BC: wool tunics, sandals, bronze armor"):

> Match the style of the attached image exactly: flat, clean illustration with bold shapes and strong contrast, like a picture book for adults. Portrait orientation. No text or lettering anywhere. Ninth-century-BC Israel: wool robes, sandals, mudbrick and stone. No crosses, nothing Greek or Roman.

The attached image is a previously accepted scene from the same season (for style) plus the cast sheet of any character who appears (for identity), with the instruction "same person, different pose."

**Composition rules.** At most eight tappable things in a moment, six is better; each large enough to read at arm's length (a rough test: nothing that matters is smaller than a tenth of the picture's width). A figure's action must be readable in the pose alone. Strong value contrast between figures and ground. The thing a case turns on is never in a corner.

**Edits over regeneration.** When one element is wrong, reply to the image with "Same picture, but …" rather than regenerating; composition drift breaks tap regions.

## 7. Picture acceptance checklist

Run before a picture enters a case.

1. Every item on the case's must-read list is present and readable at phone size.
2. Nothing on the catch list (§3) appears.
3. Every recurring character shows their marker; a stranger to the case could match them to the cast sheet.
4. No text or lettering.
5. Violence and the LORD depicted only as §5 allows.
6. Style header used; style matches the accepted scenes.
7. Nothing that matters sits in an edge zone (the outer 8% of the width) where a phone's system gestures live.

## 8. Known deviations to fix before release

- Tutorial: the boy and the shield-bearer wear gold headbands — commoners wear plain cloth.
- Tutorial: the armor heap is chain mail — regenerate as scale.
- Bedchamber: the pouch spills coins — regenerate as cut silver with a balance scale on the table.

Prototype pictures are accepted as-is for testing; each item above becomes an `art` Issue before season one ships.
